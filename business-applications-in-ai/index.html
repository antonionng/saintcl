Day 1 | Core concepts

Value chain: where AI fits in banking operations

AI is not a product. It is a capability applied within existing workflows. The value comes from where you insert it, not from the model itself.

  1. Customer acquisition: lead scoring, application pre-screening, digital onboarding.
  2. Operations: document processing, reconciliation, exception handling.
  3. Risk and compliance: transaction monitoring, credit decisioning support, regulatory reporting.
  4. Customer service: inquiry routing, response drafting, sentiment analysis.

A mid-size Gulf bank deployed an AI routing system for its contact centre. Inbound inquiries were classified by topic and urgency, then routed to the right team with a suggested response draft.

  • Average handling time dropped 22% within three months.
  • First-contact resolution improved from 61% to 74%.
  • Staff reported less time on repetitive categorization, more time on complex cases.

The key enabler was not the model. It was clean labelling of historical inquiry data over six months of preparation.

  • False positive rate dropped 35%, reducing investigator workload.

  • Model outputs were paired with human review before any action was taken.

What these cases have in common

  1. The business problem was defined before the technology was chosen.

Day 1 | Core concepts

AI washing: recognising hype

AI washing is the practice of labelling a project as "AI" when it is really a simple rule, a basic dashboard, or a rebadged analytics query. It wastes budget and erodes credibility.

  • Signal: the project cannot articulate what the model learns from data.
  • Signal: there is no feedback loop or retraining plan.
  • Signal: "AI" is used to justify budget, not to describe a real capability.

Your job is to distinguish genuine AI opportunity from branding.

Day 1 | Core concepts

Common failure modes

Organisational failures

No executive sponsor. No workflow owner. Misaligned success metrics between data team and business unit. No change management plan.

Technical failures

Training data does not represent production. Model drift goes unmonitored. Integration with existing systems is underestimated. No fallback when model is unavailable.

Day 1 | Core concepts

The business problem statement

Before any AI assessment, the business problem must be stated in plain language. A good problem statement answers four questions.

  1. What is the current workflow and who owns it?
  2. What outcome is unsatisfactory today, and how is it measured?
  3. What would "better" look like in specific, measurable terms?
  4. Who is affected: staff, customers, regulators, or all three?

Day 1 | Core concepts

Operating model choices

AI can be inserted into a workflow in three ways. The right choice depends on risk, explainability, and the bank's capacity to govern the output.

Augment

AI provides recommendations. A human reviews and decides. Best for high-risk, regulated decisions. Example: credit scoring support.

Automate

AI executes the task end-to-end within defined rules. Human oversight via exception review. Example: document data extraction.

Customer-facing

AI interacts directly with the customer. Requires highest governance bar. Example: chatbot for account inquiries.

Day 1 | Core concepts

Data readiness: the prerequisite

No data, no AI. Before recommending any AI initiative, assess data availability honestly.

  • Availability: Does the data exist in a usable format? Is it accessible?
  • Quality: How complete, accurate, and consistent is it?
  • Volume: Is there enough to train and validate a model?
  • Labelling: For supervised learning, are outcomes labelled reliably?
  • Freshness: How current is the data? Does it reflect present conditions?

Opportunity Assessment Framework

A structured method for identifying, scoring, and ranking AI opportunities. The framework ensures consistency and reduces the influence of enthusiasm over evidence.

Day 1 | Assessment framework

The opportunity mapping sequence

  1. Generate a long list of candidate workflows.
  2. Describe each candidate with a standard template.
  3. Score each on value, feasibility, and risk (1-5 scale).
  4. Plot opportunities on a prioritisation matrix.
  5. Select the top 2-3 for deeper assessment.
  6. Write a recommendation for the strongest candidate.

Day 1 | Assessment framework

Step 3: scoring criteria

Score each opportunity on three dimensions using a 1-5 scale.

Value (1-5)

1 = marginal improvement. 3 = meaningful efficiency gain. 5 = transformative business impact with clear SAR/hour savings.

Feasibility (1-5)

1 = no data, no owner. 3 = data exists, needs preparation. 5 = data ready, workflow documented, owner engaged.

Risk (1-5, lower is better)

1 = internal tool, low exposure. 3 = customer-adjacent, moderate review. 5 = direct customer impact, regulatory sensitivity.

Plot opportunities on a 2x2 matrix with value on the vertical axis and feasibility on the horizontal axis. Size each bubble by risk (larger = higher risk).

Low valueAvoid (low return, high effort)Quick wins (do if low risk)

The best first AI project sits in the top-right quadrant with a small risk bubble.

Day 1 | Assessment framework

Worked example: scoring rationale

  • Value = 4: At 800 complaints/day and 4 minutes saved per complaint, the annual saving is approximately 8,500 staff hours. This frees capacity for complex case handling.
  • Feasibility = 4: Three years of labelled data in a structured CRM. The workflow owner is identified and supportive. Integration path to existing ticketing system is clear.
  • Risk = 2: The model suggests, but does not decide. Misclassification causes a routing delay, not a regulatory breach. Human confirmation is preserved.

This opportunity sits in the top-right quadrant: high value, high feasibility, manageable risk.

Day 1 | Assessment framework

Worked example: what could go wrong

Even strong candidates carry risks. Identify them early.

  • Historical labels may be inconsistent if classification rules changed over time.
  • Model may perform poorly on new product categories not represented in training data.
  • Agent adoption may be low if the suggestion accuracy is below 85% initially.
  • Complaints in Arabic and English may need separate handling or a multilingual model.

Each risk should have a mitigation: data audit, pilot scope limits, adoption tracking, language testing.

Estimating financial impact

Executive sponsors want numbers. Use conservative, defensible estimates.

Always state your assumptions. A range (conservative to optimistic) is better than a single number.

Day 1 | Assessment framework

Comparing opportunities: trade-off analysis

When two opportunities score similarly, use these tiebreakers.

  1. Speed to pilot: Which can be tested in 8-12 weeks?
  2. Reversibility: Which is easier to stop or adjust if results are poor?
  3. Visibility: Which will build confidence in AI across the bank?
  4. Learning value: Which teaches the organisation the most about operating AI?

The best first project is often not the highest value. It is the one that builds capability for everything that follows.

Day 1 | Assessment framework

Anti-patterns in opportunity selection

  • The trophy project: Chosen for executive visibility, not for genuine value or feasibility. High risk of overpromise.
  • The pet project: Pushed by one enthusiastic individual without workflow ownership or stakeholder alignment.
  • The platform play: "Let's build an AI platform first." This delays value and creates cost without a concrete use case.
  • The copycat: "Competitor X is doing it." Without understanding their context, data, and results.

Case Discussion and Critique

Examine real-world AI deployments in banking. Identify what worked, what failed, and what governance lessons apply to AJB.

Day 2 | Case discussion

Case 1: chatbot deployment that failed

A Gulf bank launched a customer-facing chatbot for account inquiries. Within three months, it was quietly disabled.

  • The bot handled 40% of inquiries correctly but gave confidently wrong answers to the other 60%.
  • No escalation path was designed. Customers could not easily reach a human.
  • Social media complaints generated reputational damage.
  • Post-mortem: training data covered only routine inquiries; edge cases were not handled; no confidence threshold was set.

Lesson: Customer-facing AI requires the highest governance bar and a robust fallback.

Day 2 | Case discussion

Case 2: successful AML surveillance upgrade

A Southeast Asian bank implemented ML-enhanced transaction monitoring alongside its existing rules engine.

  • The model identified transaction patterns the rules engine missed, contributing to a 15% increase in genuine SAR filings.
  • False positives decreased 30%, saving approximately 12,000 investigator hours per year.
  • Every model alert went through human investigation before any action.
  • The model was retrained quarterly with feedback from investigator decisions.

Lesson: Augmentation with a strong feedback loop produces sustainable value.

Day 2 | Case discussion

Case 3: credit model bias incident

A European bank discovered that its AI credit scoring model was systematically under-scoring applicants from certain postcodes, correlating with ethnicity.

  • The model used postcode as a proxy variable. This was not intentional but emerged from training data patterns.
  • Regulatory investigation led to a fine and mandatory remediation.
  • The bank now conducts bias testing across all protected characteristics before any model deployment.

Lesson: Fairness testing is not optional. Proxy discrimination is a governance failure, not a technical accident.

Day 2 | Case discussion

Case 4: internal document processing success

A bank deployed NLP-based extraction for trade finance documents (letters of credit, bills of lading). Previously, each document required 30 minutes of manual data entry.

  • AI extraction reduced processing to 10 minutes with human verification of all extracted fields.
  • Error rate dropped from 4.2% (manual) to 1.8% (AI-assisted with human check).
  • Staff redeployed from data entry to exception handling and customer communication.

Lesson: Internal, low-risk automation with human verification is the easiest path to value.

Your recommendation must follow this structure. Each section should be concise and evidence-based.

Day 2 | Applied mission

Writing for executives: principles

  • Lead with the recommendation. State what you propose in the first paragraph. Do not build up to it.
  • Quantify where possible. Hours saved, SAR amounts, percentage improvements. Ranges are acceptable.
  • Name the risks. Executives distrust recommendations that ignore downsides.
  • Be specific about the next step. "We recommend a 12-week pilot in the CX department with a go/no-go review at week 8."
  • Keep it short. Two pages maximum. Use tables and bullet points for density.

Day 2 | Recap and close

Key principles to carry forward

  1. Start with the business problem, not the technology.
  2. Score opportunities on value, feasibility, and risk before committing resources.
  3. Choose the right operating model for the risk profile of the workflow.
  4. Design governance alongside the technical build, not after.
  5. Measure by business outcomes, not model metrics.
  6. Build capability through well-chosen first projects, not platform investments.
