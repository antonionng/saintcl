Day 1 * Foundations

Most of the bank fits inside five tables. The trap is what one row means.

Customers, accounts, transactions, branches, service tickets. You have all met them before; that is not the lesson. The lesson is that the same table name means a different thing in retail than it does in treasury, and the engineer who does not ask the grain question is the engineer who counts a settlement leg as a customer payment.

The five tables, in plain language
  • customers - the parties the bank holds a relationship with. Not the same as "people"; corporates and trusts live here too.
  • accounts - the products those parties opened. One customer can have ten; one account belongs to one customer.
  • transactions - what moved across those accounts. Not always customer-initiated. The ones that are not are where the trouble starts.
  • branches - where the relationship is anchored. Anchored, not constrained; transactions happen elsewhere.
  • service tickets - what the relationship asked for. Calls, complaints, requests; the only signal of how the customer feels.
The grain question, every time

Before you trust a number, answer the question out loud: one row in this table represents what? "One transaction" is not an answer. "One leg of one settlement, posted on a calendar date that may not be the value date" is.

I have seen a Q1 retail performance number include the corporate treasury settlement leg because nobody asked the grain question. The number was off by a factor that cost two weeks to unwind.

What each table means - and what it does not mean

TableMeansDoes NOT mean

accounts One product opened by one or more parties at a point in time. An active product. A funded product. A profitable product.
transactions An event posted to an account on a value date. A real-world economic event. A net movement. A confirmed settlement.
branches The booking branch as recorded in the source system. Where the customer banks day to day. Where the staff sit.
service tickets A request that reached the support queue. Total customer pain. Resolution quality. Repeat-contact rate.
Day 1 | Foundations

Common gotchas - the ones that quietly break analyses

Time and currency
  • Posting date vs value date vs effective date - they are not the same.
  • Reversals and back-dated entries can move yesterday’s number tomorrow.
  • Currency mixed across rows without a conversion column. Treat as flagged until proven otherwise.
Identity and absence
  • Null is not zero. Absence has to be interpreted, not coerced.
  • Duplicate ids may be re-issued ids, not true duplicates. Confirm before deduping.
  • One customer can hold many accounts. One account can have many holders. Joins drift if you forget.

For every cut

Day 1 | Lab A | Pitfalls

Common ways triage goes wrong

Confidence without evidence

"Looks clean" with no checks behind it. Always tie a verdict to a number you ran.

Sampling without saying so

Eyeballing the first 50 rows and deciding for the whole file. State your sample, or run the check on the whole file.

Missing the one issue that matters

You can have a clean structure and an analytically broken file. Always ask: would this change the call leadership has to make?

Hedging the verdict

"It depends" is not a triage. Pick a position and put the caveats in the next sentence.

Day 1 * Lab B * Definitions

Two engineers, the same KPI, different numbers. The gap is always one of these four.

N, D, E, cut-off. Numerator, denominator, exclusions, cut-off. Pick a metric you have ever shipped at this bank and I can show you the version of it that disagrees with the version next to it. The disagreement is never about pandas. It is always about one of these four.

Numerator

What event counts? "Transactions" silently includes reversals at most banks. "Net inflow" is a different number from "gross posted". Pick the one the room agrees with before you write it down.

Denominator

Active accounts at start of quarter, end of quarter, or average across? Same KPI, three rankings, three different branch managers calling you on Monday.

Exclusions

Internal transfers, dormant accounts, test accounts, staff accounts. Each one is a five-second decision now or a forty-minute defence later.

Cut-off

Posting date or value date? Time zone? Late-arriving rows: include or freeze the snapshot? Late on Sunday is in the next week if you used posting date and in the right week if you used value date.

Loose definitions are not a writing problem. They are an engineering risk. The hour you spend pinning these is the day you do not spend defending the number.

Day 1 | Lab B | Step 3 - Verify

Recompute under one alternative denominator

The verify move

Take the same numerator. Swap in one alternative denominator (for example: active accounts at quarter-end vs accounts open at quarter-start). Compute both. Compare the rankings.

What to look at
  • Which branches move ranks under the swap?
  • By how much - one place, or top-three to mid-pack?
  • Which version is more honest about what last quarter actually was?

If both rankings are stable, the metric is robust. If they are not, you have a definition problem and the choice is yours to defend.

Day 1 | Lab B | Trap

The two-trustworthy-versions trap

Both versions are technically correct

The numerator is the same. The denominator changed. Both queries ran. Both produced a ranked table. Both can be defended in isolation.

The trap is treating the choice as arbitrary. It is not. The choice is the thing that gets challenged. The choice is what you defend in front of the regional lead.

Pick the one that survives the question "what does this metric mean for a branch that opened mid-quarter?"

Day 2 * Lab C * What a pack actually is

If it needs a verbal walkthrough, it is not a pack. It is a script.

An executive pack is what survives the meeting you are not in. The CFO opens it on Friday morning, reads it once, makes a decision. You will not be in the room to explain the chart, defend the choice, or tell her where the caveat lives. So the pack has to do all three by itself.

A headline that names the trend

"Top-quartile branches grew 12%; bottom-quartile flat" beats "Q1 KPI ranked". The headline says what changed; the table only says what is. A title is not a headline.

Two charts that prove it

One chart shows the headline. One chart shows the spread that makes the headline resilient to "yes, but is it everywhere?". Two charts; not three. Three is hedging.

An exception view that pre-empts

Three to five branches the headline does not describe. Named, reasoned, on the same page. The CFO will ask. Have the answer in front of her before she does.

The caveat travels with the headline. A caveat in the appendix is liability management, not engineering.

Day 2 * Lab D * What ML-ready means

ML-ready means the next engineer never has to come and find you to ask a question.

"ML-ready" is a phrase analysts use loosely and ML engineers use precisely. Today we use it the way the ML engineer uses it. Three things have to be true. None of them are about pandas. All of them are about contract.

Cut-off is explicit

A single date written at the top of the brief. Nothing in the table is computed using anything that happened after that date. Not "roughly". Not "as of around". A date. Non-negotiable.

Leakage is impossible

No column uses a fact that did not exist at the cut-off. The classic leak is a "churn flag" computed today and joined to a feature table from six months ago. The model will look brilliant. It will fail in production.

Contract is written

One row = one customer at the cut-off. Every column has a dictionary entry: name, definition, source, cut-off-aware logic, caveat. The dictionary is the contract. Without it, the table is folklore.

The model the next team builds on top of this either works because of the contract or fails because of its absence. There is no middle outcome.

Day 2 * Lab D * Dictionary

The dictionary is the only artefact that outlives the engineer who built the table.

You will leave this team. The model team will pick up your work without you. The dictionary is the contract that survives that handoff. Coach-voice dictionaries read like definitions in a textbook; engineer-voice dictionaries read like something the next engineer can actually act on. We want the second.

Per-feature, in your voice
  • Name. snake_case. No abbreviations a new joiner would not recognise on day one.
  • Definition. One sentence. What the value represents in the world, not how it was computed.
  • Source. Raw column(s) and the join that produced it. The next engineer should be able to retrace it without you in the room.
  • Cut-off behaviour. What time window is included. What is excluded. Be specific about edge cases.
  • Caveat. One thing the modeller should know before using this as a feature. The thing they would otherwise discover painfully.
Coach voice vs engineer voice

Coach voice (do not ship this):
