Day 1 | Chart Selection

Chart selection principles

The right chart makes the pattern obvious. The wrong chart hides it or distorts it.

Chart selection is not about aesthetics. It is about matching the visual encoding to the analytical task: comparison, composition, distribution, relationship, or change over time.

Day 1 | Comparison

Comparing categories: bar charts

Use horizontal or vertical bars when the task is "which category is largest?" or "how do categories rank?"

  • Horizontal bars work best when category labels are long (branch names, product names).
  • Always start the value axis at zero. Truncated axes distort perception of difference.
  • Sort bars by value unless the categories have a natural order (months, ratings).

AJB example: comparing revenue across 15 branches, sorted highest to lowest.

Day 1 | Trends

Showing change over time: line charts

Lines connect data points in sequence, making trends, seasonality, and inflection points visible.

  • Use lines only when the x-axis represents a continuous sequence (time, distance).
  • Limit to 4-5 lines per chart. Beyond that, use small multiples.
  • Highlight the line that matters most with colour or thickness; mute the rest.

AJB example: monthly transaction volume trends across three regions over 24 months.

Day 1 | Relationships

Exploring relationships: scatter plots

Scatter plots reveal correlation, clusters, and outliers between two continuous variables.

  • Each point represents one observation (a branch, a customer segment, a product).
  • Use colour or size to encode a third variable (region, revenue tier).
  • Add a trend line only when the relationship is meaningful, not to decorate.

AJB example: plotting customer satisfaction against digital adoption rate by segment.

Day 1 | Composition

Showing parts of a whole: composition charts

Stacked bars

Good for comparing composition across categories. Use when there are 2-4 segments. More segments make stacked bars unreadable.

Treemaps

Good for showing hierarchical composition at a glance. Use when you have nested categories (region > branch > product).

Avoid pie charts for precise comparison. Humans estimate angles poorly. Reserve pie charts for cases with only 2-3 segments where the message is "this one dominates."

Day 1 | Distribution

Understanding distribution: histograms and box plots

Distribution charts answer: "What is the spread? Where do most values concentrate? Are there outliers?"

  • Histograms show frequency across bins. Choose bin width carefully: too few bins hide patterns, too many create noise.
  • Box plots summarize median, quartiles, and outliers compactly. Useful for comparing distributions across groups.
  • Violin plots combine distribution shape with summary statistics.

AJB example: distribution of average customer balances across all branches.

Day 1 | Heatmaps

Density and intensity: heatmaps

Heatmaps use colour intensity to encode values in a matrix, making large datasets scannable.

  • Best for showing patterns across two categorical dimensions (branch vs month, product vs region).
  • Use a sequential colour scale (light to dark) for single-direction data.
  • Use a diverging colour scale (red-white-green) when there is a meaningful centre point (target, zero, average).

AJB example: monthly satisfaction scores across all branches as a heatmap grid.

Day 1 | Visual Hierarchy

Visual hierarchy: directing attention

If everything is emphasised, nothing is emphasised.

Visual hierarchy uses size, position, colour, and contrast to create an order of importance. The viewer should grasp the main message within 3 seconds.

  • The top-left position gets seen first in left-to-right cultures.
  • Larger elements attract attention before smaller ones.
  • Saturated colours pop against muted backgrounds.
  • White space separates ideas and reduces cognitive load.
Day 1 | Emphasis Techniques

Techniques for emphasis

Colour

Use one accent colour for the focal element. Grey out everything else. This is the single most effective emphasis technique.

Annotation

Add a direct label or callout to the key data point. Do not rely on the legend alone. Tell the viewer what to see.

Isolation

Separate the key element with white space or position. A single number in a large space draws immediate attention.

Day 1 | Annotation Best Practices

Annotation: telling the viewer what to see

Good annotations transform a chart from "here is data" to "here is the insight."

  • Label the key data point directly on the chart, not just in the legend.
  • Add a short narrative annotation: "Customer growth accelerated after the mobile launch in Q3."
  • Use reference lines to show targets, benchmarks, or regulatory thresholds.
  • Date and source every chart. An undated chart loses credibility instantly.
Day 1 | Colour Theory

Colour theory for banking dashboards

Colour is the most powerful and most misused visual channel.

  • Use a restrained palette: 1-2 accent colours plus grey. More colours create visual noise.
  • Ensure sufficient contrast for accessibility (WCAG AA minimum). Test with a colour-blindness simulator.
  • Avoid red/green pairs as the sole differentiator: 8% of men have red-green colour vision deficiency.
  • Use AJB brand colours for emphasis, not for every element. Brand saturation reduces impact.
  • Cultural note: in Saudi financial context, green typically signals positive performance. Be consistent.

Day 1 | Chart Junk

Chart junk: what to remove

Chart junk is any visual element that does not convey data. It wastes attention and reduces trust.

  • 3D effects: distort perception of size and position. Always use 2D.
  • Heavy gridlines: compete with data. Use light grey or remove entirely.
  • Decorative icons: distract from the pattern. Use only when they aid comprehension (e.g., a small map icon for geographic data).
  • Redundant legends: if you can label directly, remove the legend.
  • Background images or textures: never.
Day 1 | Misleading Scales

Misleading scales and distortions

Scale manipulation is the most common way charts deceive, intentionally or not.

  • Truncated y-axis on bar charts exaggerates differences. A 2% difference looks like 200%.
  • Dual y-axes with different scales create false correlations. Avoid them or label aggressively.
  • Inconsistent time intervals (mixing daily and monthly) distort trends.
  • Cherry-picked date ranges hide context. Always show enough history for the pattern to be fair.
If you have to explain why the scale is not misleading, the scale is misleading.
Day 1 | Typography and Labels

Typography and labelling standards

  • Use a single sans-serif font family. Mixing fonts creates visual noise.
  • Chart titles: 14-16pt, bold. Subtitle: 11-12pt, regular weight.
  • Axis labels: include units (SAR millions, %, count). Never leave axes unlabelled.
  • Data labels: use sparingly. Label only the data points that matter most.
  • Number formatting: use thousands separators (1,250,000 not 1250000). Round to appropriate precision.
  • Dates: use consistent format throughout (e.g., "Jan 2025" or "2025-01", not both).

Day 2: Dashboard architecture and composition

A dashboard is not a collection of charts. It is a visual argument with a reading order.

Today we move from individual charts to composed views: dashboards that lead the eye, geospatial analysis that reveals regional patterns, and interactive design that serves rather than distracts.

Day 2 | Dashboard Purpose

What makes a dashboard effective?

An effective dashboard answers one primary question at a glance and supports drill-down into supporting detail.

  • It has a clear reading order: summary first, then breakdown, then detail.
  • It uses no more than 5-7 visual elements. More creates cognitive overload.
  • Every element earns its space. If removing an element does not reduce understanding, remove it.
  • It is designed for a specific audience, not for "everyone."
Day 2 | Dashboard Anatomy

Anatomy of a banking dashboard

Top row: KPI tiles

3-5 key metrics with current value, trend direction, and comparison to target. These answer "how are we doing right now?"

Middle: primary visual

The largest chart answers the main analytical question. This is where the eye should spend the most time.

Supporting visuals

1-2 smaller charts that provide context, breakdown, or comparison. They support the primary visual, not compete with it.

Bottom: detail table

For users who need granular data. Sortable, filterable, but not the focal point.

Day 2 | KPI Tile Design

Designing effective KPI tiles

KPI tiles are the most-viewed element on any dashboard. Design them with care.

  • Show the current value prominently. This is the primary information.
  • Include a comparison: vs target, vs last period, or vs benchmark.
  • Use a small sparkline or arrow to show direction. Context matters more than a raw number.
  • Use colour sparingly: green for on-track, amber for warning, red for off-track. Do not colour everything.
  • Label the time period. "Revenue: SAR 18.5M" means nothing without "Q4 2025 YTD."
Day 2 | Reading Order

Designing the reading order

The viewer's eye follows a predictable path. Design your dashboard to match it.

  1. Top-left: start here. Place the most important summary element.
  2. Top-right: secondary summary or trend indicator.
  3. Centre: the primary analytical visual gets the most space.
  4. Bottom: supporting detail and data tables for drill-down.
If your dashboard requires a user guide, it has failed. The layout itself should teach the reading order.
Day 2 | White Space

White space is not wasted space

White space (or negative space) is a structural element, not a gap to fill.

  • It separates logical groups and creates visual breathing room.
  • Overcrowded dashboards cause the viewer to disengage. Cognitive overload shuts down analysis.
  • A dashboard with 4 clear visuals and generous spacing outperforms one with 10 visuals crammed together.
  • Use consistent margins and padding. Inconsistent spacing looks unprofessional.
Day 2 | Geospatial Fundamentals

Geospatial visualization fundamentals

Maps are powerful when location matters. They are distracting when it does not.

Use a map when the geographical pattern is the insight: regional concentration, branch coverage gaps, customer density gradients. If the data does not have a meaningful geographic dimension, a bar chart is clearer.

  • Choropleth maps: shade regions by value. Good for market share, growth rates.
  • Point maps: plot individual locations. Good for branch networks, customer clusters.
  • Bubble maps: size markers by value. Good for showing magnitude at each location.

Day 2 | Map Design Principles

Map design principles

  • Choose the right projection. For Saudi Arabia, a simple plate carree or Mercator works for regional views.
  • Use a sequential colour scale for single-variable choropleths. Avoid rainbow colour maps.
  • Size bubbles by area, not radius. Radius scaling exaggerates differences.
  • Include a legend with clear value ranges.
  • Do not overload the map. Show one variable at a time. Use small multiples for comparisons.
  • Always include a title and source. Maps without context are just shapes.

Risk heatmap

Day 2 | Overcrowded Dashboards

Common mistake: the overcrowded dashboard

The most frequent dashboard failure is trying to show everything to everyone.

  • More than 7 visual elements causes cognitive overload. The viewer skims and misses the key message.
  • Multiple audiences with different questions need different dashboards, not one mega-dashboard.
  • If a dashboard takes more than 10 seconds to find the main message, it needs redesign.
The goal is not to show all the data. The goal is to show the right data to the right person at the right time.

Day 2 | Colour Palettes for Dashboards

Building a dashboard colour palette

A consistent colour palette creates visual coherence across all dashboard elements.

  • Primary: 1 brand colour for accent and key data points.
  • Supporting: 2-3 muted tones for secondary data series.
  • Semantic: green (positive), amber (warning), red (negative). Use consistently across all charts.
  • Neutral: light greys for gridlines, borders, and non-data elements.
  • Background: white or very light grey. Dark backgrounds are harder to read in printed reports.

Document your palette. Reuse it across every chart in the dashboard for visual unity.

Day 2 | Accessibility

Accessibility in data visualization

Accessible visuals reach more people and meet professional standards.

  • Use shape and pattern in addition to colour. Dashed vs solid lines, different marker shapes.
  • Maintain a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for graphical elements.
  • Provide alt text for charts in digital reports. Describe the insight, not the chart type.
  • Test with a colour-blindness simulator (e.g., Coblis, Sim Daltonism).
  • Avoid relying solely on red/green to encode meaning. Add icons or labels as redundant cues.
Day 2 | Small Multiples

Small multiples: comparing without clutter

Small multiples show the same chart repeated for each category, making comparison easy without overlapping lines or bars.

  • Each panel uses identical axes. This ensures fair comparison across categories.
  • Best for 4-12 categories. Fewer than 4 wastes space; more than 12 overwhelms.
  • Label each panel clearly. The viewer should not have to decode colour from a legend.

AJB example: a small-multiple line chart showing monthly transaction trends for each region side by side.

Day 2 | Tables vs Charts

When tables beat charts

Charts are not always the answer. Tables are better when:

  • The audience needs exact values, not patterns (regulatory reports, audit evidence).
  • The dataset has fewer than 5 rows and the comparison is straightforward.
  • The data includes mixed types (names, dates, amounts, statuses) that do not map to a single visual encoding.
  • The audience will reference the data repeatedly (lookup tables, rate cards).

Use conditional formatting (colour bars, icons) to add visual pattern to tables without losing precision.

Day 2 | Critique Methodology

Visual critique methodology

Critique is a structured practice, not personal opinion.

Use the four-question critique framework for any visualization:

  1. Message: What is the first thing you see? Is that the intended message?
  2. Accuracy: Is the data represented honestly? Are scales, labels, and comparisons fair?
  3. Clarity: Can the audience understand this in under 10 seconds without explanation?
  4. Action: Does this visual lead to a decision or just present data?
Day 2 | Critique Practice

Critique practice: common failures

Problem: spaghetti line chart

12 overlapping lines, no hierarchy, rainbow colours, no annotations. Fix: reduce to 3-4 key lines, grey the rest, highlight the story line.

Problem: pie chart with 15 slices

Slices smaller than 3% are unreadable. Fix: show top 5 as bars, group the rest as "Other." Precision matters more than completeness.

Day 2 | Data-Ink Ratio

Tufte's data-ink ratio

Edward Tufte's principle: maximise the share of ink devoted to data. Minimise non-data ink.

  • Data-ink ratio = ink used for data / total ink on the chart.
  • Remove borders around charts unless they serve a grouping purpose.
  • Remove background fills, shadows, and gradients.
  • Reduce gridlines to the minimum needed for value estimation.
  • Every pixel should either show data or help the viewer interpret data.

Day 2 | Interactivity Principles

Principles of useful interactivity

Interactivity should clarify the story, not create extra work for the audience.

  • Default view must be meaningful. The dashboard on first load should answer the primary question without any clicks.
  • Filters should reduce, not multiply. Each filter should narrow the view, not create new confusion.
  • Tooltips provide detail on demand. They should show values, comparisons, and context, not repeat the axis label.
  • Drill-down should follow the analytical hierarchy: summary to segment to individual.
  • Undo is essential. The user must always be able to return to the default view.
Day 2 | Filter Design

Designing effective filters

  • Place filters at the top or left. They should be visible but not dominate the view.
  • Show the current filter state clearly. The viewer must always know what subset of data they are seeing.
  • Limit filter options to meaningful categories. Do not expose every column as a filter.
  • Use cascading filters: selecting a region should update the branch filter to show only branches in that region.
  • Provide a "Reset all" button prominently. Users get lost in filter combinations.
Day 2 | Advanced Chart Types

Advanced chart types for banking

Waterfall

Shows how an initial value is affected by sequential positive and negative changes. Ideal for bridge analyses: revenue drivers, cost breakdown.

Bullet chart

Compact comparison of actual vs target with qualitative ranges (poor, satisfactory, good). Replaces gauges and dials.

Sankey diagram

Shows flow between categories. Useful for customer journey mapping, fund flow analysis, channel attribution.

Day 2 | Sparklines and Micro-Charts

Sparklines and micro-charts

Sparklines are small, word-sized charts embedded in text or tables. They show trend without taking dashboard space.

  • Use in KPI tiles to show the trend behind the number.
  • Embed in tables to add a visual dimension to rows of numbers.
  • Remove axes, labels, and gridlines. The shape is the message.
  • Highlight the start point, end point, and minimum/maximum if relevant.

AJB example: a branch performance table with inline sparklines showing 12-month revenue trends per branch.

Day 2 | Conditional Formatting

Conditional formatting in data tables

Tables with conditional formatting bridge the gap between precision and pattern recognition.

  • Colour bars: show relative magnitude within a column. Quick visual comparison.
  • Icon sets: arrows (up/down/flat), traffic lights (green/amber/red). Use for status indicators.
  • Highlight rules: bold or colour cells that exceed thresholds. "Churn rate above 5% turns red."
  • Keep formatting consistent across the dashboard. Mixed formatting standards look chaotic.
Day 2 | Narrative Flow

Building narrative flow across multiple visuals

When a dashboard or report contains multiple visuals, they should tell a connected story.

  1. Start with the "so what": the summary that frames the context.
  2. Follow with the evidence: the charts that support the headline.
  3. Close with the implication: what should the audience do next?

Each chart should answer a question raised by the previous one. If a chart does not connect to the narrative, remove it.

Day 3 | Executive Communication

Executive communication principles

Executives have limited time and high standards. Your visual communication must respect both.

  • Lead with the conclusion. State the key finding first, then show the evidence. Do not build up to a reveal.
  • One slide, one message. If a slide makes two points, split it into two slides.
  • Use action titles: "Eastern region growth outpaces all others at 10.1%" not "Regional Growth Chart."
  • Anticipate questions. Include the data that supports the likely follow-up question.
  • End with a clear recommendation, not an open question.
Day 3 | Reporting Pack Structure

Structure of an executive reporting pack

  1. Executive summary (1 page): 3-5 KPIs, headline finding, recommended action.
  2. Performance overview (1-2 pages): Trend charts, comparison to targets, variance analysis.
  3. Deep dive (2-3 pages): Segment analysis, regional breakdown, root cause investigation.
  4. Appendix: Methodology, data sources, definitions. Available for reference, not for presentation.

Total: 5-7 pages maximum. If you cannot tell the story in 7 pages, you have not identified the story yet.

Day 3 | Storytelling Structures

Storytelling structures for data

Situation-Complication-Resolution

Describe the current state (situation), identify the challenge or surprise (complication), and present the recommendation (resolution). Classic consulting structure.

What-So What-Now What

Present the finding (what), explain why it matters (so what), and recommend next steps (now what). Direct and efficient for operational audiences.

Day 3 | Common Presentation Mistakes

Common mistakes in data presentations

  • Reading the chart aloud. The audience can read. Add interpretation and context instead.
  • Showing the data journey. The audience does not need to know how you cleaned the data or which tools you used.
  • Ending with "any questions?" instead of a recommendation. Close with a call to action.
  • Using jargon the audience does not share. Match your language to their vocabulary.
  • Showing too many decimal places. SAR 18,523,417.83 is noise. SAR 18.5M is the message.

Day 3 | Presentation Practice

Presenting your executive pack

Present your reporting pack in 3 minutes or less. Structure:

  1. 30 seconds: State the headline finding and recommendation.
  2. 90 seconds: Walk through 2-3 key visuals that support the finding.
  3. 30 seconds: Restate the recommendation with the most compelling data point.
  4. 30 seconds: Open for one question.

Do not describe the chart type or your methodology. Describe the business insight.

Day 3 | Presentation Anti-Patterns

Presentation anti-patterns

  • "This chart shows..." (describing the visual instead of the insight)
  • "As you can see..." (assuming the audience has already processed the visual)
  • "I used matplotlib to..." (sharing technical process, not business value)
  • "The data is interesting because..." (vague; state the specific finding)
  • Turning your back to the audience to read the screen.
  • Showing a chart and then saying something different from what the chart shows.
Day 3 | Module Recap

Module recap: key principles

Selection

Match the chart to the analytical task. Bars for comparison, lines for trends, scatter for relationships, heatmaps for density.

Hierarchy

Use colour, size, annotation, and position to direct attention. Make the main message land in 3 seconds.

Composition

Dashboards have a reading order. KPI tiles first, primary chart centre, supporting detail below.
