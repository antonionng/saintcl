Day 1 | Regulation

What changes in a regulated environment

In banking, accuracy is not the only question. You also need auditability, fairness, clear ownership, data lineage, monitoring, and escalation paths when the model behaves unexpectedly.

  • Can we explain how the score influences action?
  • Can we show what data was used and when?
  • Can we detect drift, failure, or bias quickly?
  • Can a human intervene before harm scales?

Day 1 | Problem framing

What makes a good ML problem statement

A good ML problem statement is decision-led. It states what action will change, what the model predicts, over what time horizon, for which unit, and what trade-off matters most.

Example

"Predict which service tickets are likely to breach SLA within 24 hours so supervisors can intervene earlier, with recall prioritised over precision."

Day 1 | Fundamentals

Features, labels, targets, and observations

Observation

One row. A customer, account, transaction window, or support case.

Label or target

The outcome you want to predict, such as churn within 90 days.

Features are the input columns used to predict the label. They must exist before the prediction moment, not after it.

Business context before modelling

Day 1 | Splits

Train, validation, and test sets

The training set teaches the model. The validation set helps you compare and tune. The test set is the final untouched check of likely real-world performance.

Discipline

If you use the test set repeatedly while tuning, it stops being a real test and becomes part of the training process.

Day 1 | Leakage

Why leakage breaks trust

Leakage happens when your model sees information that would not exist at prediction time. It creates scores that look impressive in development but collapse in production.

  • Using post-outcome data in the feature table
  • Aggregations that look ahead in time
  • Data preparation steps fitted on the full dataset
Day 1 | Leakage examples

Leakage examples in banking datasets

Bad feature

"Collections contact count in the next 30 days" when predicting default risk today.

Bad join

Joining a complaint status updated after escalation into a model predicting which tickets escalate.

Day 1 | Missing data

Missing data strategies for ML

Drop

Use when missingness is tiny and low-risk.

Impute

Use median, mode, or domain logic carefully.

Flag

Sometimes missingness itself is informative.

Day 1 | Encoding

Encoding categories carefully

Models need numeric input. Categorical features like branch region or account type must be encoded, but encoding choices can create false order or unhelpful sparsity if used carelessly.

  • One-hot encoding for most nominal categories
  • Ordinal encoding only when order is real
  • Group rare categories before explosion
Day 1 | Scaling

Scaling and standardisation. When it matters

Distance-based and gradient-based models often behave better when features are on comparable scales. Tree-based models usually care much less.

Rule of thumb

Scale for logistic regression, k-nearest neighbours, SVM, and k-means. It is usually optional for decision trees and random forests.

Day 1 | Baselines

Baselines before sophistication

A baseline forces you to ask whether complexity is actually earning its keep. If a simple rule or simple model performs well enough, it may be the safer operational choice.

  • Majority class baseline
  • Simple logistic model
  • Current manual rule benchmark

Day 1 | Metrics

Accuracy is not enough

In imbalanced banking problems, accuracy can be misleading. A model can look strong while missing most of the rare but important cases.

Example

If only 2% of cases are fraud, a model that predicts "not fraud" every time is 98% accurate and still useless.

Day 1 | Metrics

Precision, recall, and F1

Precision

Of the cases we flagged, how many were truly positive?

Recall

Of all true positives, how many did we catch?

F1

A balance measure when precision and recall both matter.

Day 1 | Thresholds

Thresholds and business trade-offs

Many models produce probabilities. The threshold decides when you act. Lowering it usually catches more positives but also creates more false alarms.

  • Collections may prefer higher recall
  • Fraud operations may tune by alert handling capacity
  • Customer messaging may require higher precision
Day 1 | Error cost

False positives and false negatives in banking

False positive

You intervene on the wrong case. Cost may be customer friction, wasted analyst time, or revenue loss.

False negative

You miss the case you needed to catch. Cost may be fraud loss, default, churn, or SLA breach.

Day 1 | Ranking metrics

ROC and PR curve intuition

ROC curves are useful when classes are balanced enough to compare ranking quality. Precision-recall curves become especially important when the positive class is rare and operational focus is on the positives.

Day 2 | Feature design

Feature selection for segmentation

Good segmentation features describe stable behaviour or value. Bad segmentation features simply reproduce noise, one-off campaigns, or identifiers.

Examples

Average balance, digital activity rate, product diversity, complaint frequency, salary inflow regularity, and branch dependence.

Day 2 | Intuition

Distance and similarity intuition

Many clustering methods group observations that are close together in feature space. If scales are inconsistent or noisy features dominate, the groups become hard to trust.

Day 2 | K-means

K-means explained simply

K-means starts with candidate centres, assigns each observation to the nearest centre, then updates centres repeatedly until assignments stabilise.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=4, random_state=42)
clusters = kmeans.fit_predict(X_scaled)
Day 2 | Model choice

Choosing the number of clusters

  • Use elbow and silhouette methods as input, not as final truth.
  • Prefer solutions that are interpretable and operationally distinct.
  • A mathematically neat segmentation can still be useless in business.
Day 2 | Interpretation

Interpreting clusters for business use

Weak interpretation

Cluster 2 has higher values on features 4 and 7.

Strong interpretation

This cluster represents digitally active salary customers with low service friction and high cross-sell potential.

Day 2 | Risk

Cluster risk and misuse

Clusters are descriptive, not moral categories. Do not treat them as permanent truths or use them without checking for sensitive bias, instability, or weak business meaning.

Day 2 | Simplicity

Linear and logistic models. When simplicity wins

Simple models are often easier to audit, explain, and monitor. In regulated settings, a modestly weaker but far clearer model may still be the better choice.

Day 2 | Trees

Tree-based models. Why they are popular

  • Handle nonlinear patterns well
  • Work with mixed feature types
  • Need less feature scaling
  • Often provide strong practical baselines
Day 2 | Ensembles

Ensemble intuition. Random forest and boosting

Ensembles combine many trees to improve stability or predictive power. They are powerful, but the governance burden also rises because their decisions are harder to explain end to end.

Day 2 | Trade-offs

Bias variance trade-off

High bias means the model is too simple to capture the signal. High variance means the model is too sensitive to the training data. Strong model selection is about managing both.

Day 2 | Fit quality

Underfitting and overfitting

Underfitting

Poor performance on train and test. The model never learned enough.

Overfitting

Strong training performance, weak test performance. The model learned the noise.

Day 2 | Validation

Cross-validation discipline

Cross-validation reduces the risk of being fooled by one lucky split. It gives a more stable sense of model performance, especially when datasets are not huge.

Day 2 | Fair comparison

Comparing models fairly

  • Use the same split logic
  • Use the same target and horizon
  • Hold preprocessing discipline constant
  • Compare against the same metric and threshold assumptions
Day 2 | Interpretation

Feature importance. Promise and limits

Feature importance can help explain a model, but it does not automatically prove causality. It shows what the model used, not what the business should assume is truly driving behaviour.

Day 2 | Explainability

Explainability versus predictive strength

Sometimes the most powerful model is not the best operating choice. If explainability is required for review, challenge, or customer impact, a slightly weaker but clearer model may be more valuable.

Day 2 | Governance

Governance questions every model owner must answer

  1. What decision does the model change?
  2. What data enters the model and from where?
  3. How is performance measured and monitored?
  4. Who owns action when the model drifts or fails?
  5. What human review exists for sensitive cases?
Day 2 | Fairness

Fairness and bias in banking decisions

Bias can enter through the target, features, labels, sampling, operational history, or intervention policy. Fairness review should happen before deployment and continue after go-live.

Day 2 | Monitoring

Concept drift

Outcome drift

Day 2 | Human oversight

Human oversight and escalation points

Not every model output should lead directly to action. High-impact decisions often need review queues, override logic, and documented escalation rules.

Day 2 | Risk management

Day 3 | Deployment

What deployment actually means in practice

  1. Move code and data logic into a controlled environment.
  2. Define interfaces, schedules, and failure handling.
  3. Connect scores to workflow or queue logic.
  4. Monitor health, performance, and user response.
Day 3 | Delivery model

Batch scoring versus real-time scoring

Batch

Good for overnight prioritisation, campaigns, and periodic review lists.

Real time

Good for fraud, alerts, routing, or decisions that must happen during interaction.

Day 3 | Production assumptions

Input contracts and production assumptions

Production systems need explicit contracts. Which fields must arrive? Which can be missing? What ranges are valid? What should happen when a feed breaks?

Day 3 | Monitoring

Monitoring metrics after go-live

  • Input quality and schema health
  • Score distribution stability
  • Prediction outcome quality over time
  • Operational queue size and action rate
  • Business outcome lift versus baseline
Day 3 | Retraining

Feedback loops and retraining triggers

Retraining should be triggered by evidence, not by habit alone. Drift, degraded performance, new policy, or changed product strategy may all require a refresh.

Day 3 | Failure modes

Model failure modes in banking operations

Data failure

Broken feeds, stale extracts, schema changes.

Logic failure

Unexpected feature distributions or hidden leakage.

Operational failure

Scores generated but no one acts on them.

Day 3 | Resilience

Incident handling and rollback logic

When a model behaves badly, the business needs a clear response: alert, investigate, switch to fallback rules, document the event, and review before returning to service.

Day 3 | Third-party models

Responsible use of third-party models

Outsourcing the model does not outsource accountability. The bank still needs clarity on data use, drift handling, explainability, human review, and service-level commitments.

Day 3 | Vendor scrutiny

Vendor questions for ML platforms

  1. How is performance measured and monitored?
  2. What data leaves the bank?
  3. What explainability artifacts are available?
  4. What rollback and support process exists?
  5. What evidence supports fairness and robustness?
Day 3 | Executive communication

How to communicate model results to non-technical leaders

Leaders do not need every parameter. They need to know what decision changes, how much confidence to place in the result, what risks remain, and what governance surrounds the model.
